## 02 — What does home intelligence actually change?

Home intelligence is the control layer that lets a residence behave like one system instead of a collection of unrelated products. DAVG designs and programs a local-first Control4 architecture so the actions people repeat every day—arriving, entertaining, watching, leaving, and going to sleep—can happen with one deliberate command.

The result is not more technology to manage. It is less friction: fewer apps, fewer remotes, fewer repeated adjustments, and clearer control from keypads, touchscreens, and personal devices.

## 03 — The moments to automate

Press **Arrive** and the entry, kitchen, and living spaces welcome you at the right light levels. Start **Dinner** and music settles into the rooms that matter without spilling through the entire house. Select **Goodnight** and the home confirms lighting, shades, climate, doors, and active media from one place.

The best automation feels predictable, not theatrical. It respects manual control, makes the common actions obvious, and keeps advanced choices available without placing them in everyone’s way.

## 04 — How the system connects

The experience begins with a reliable digital foundation and a clear responsibility map. Control, lighting, shades, climate, media, and security are specified as coordinated subsystems; the network, rack, power, wiring, and interfaces are designed to support them together.

Core household functions should remain available locally when internet service is interrupted. Cloud services can add remote access and selected conveniences, but they should not become an unnecessary dependency for basic in-home operation.

### What is Control4, in plain English?

Control4 is the operating and orchestration layer for compatible systems in the residence. A controller runs the automation project; drivers translate between Control4 and connected equipment; interfaces let people act; the network carries communication; and the integrator designs the logic that turns separate systems into predictable household behavior.

It is important to separate **control** from **connectivity**. A device appearing in an app does not mean it has been integrated into a resilient whole-home architecture. Integration requires defined states, tested dependencies, understandable fallback behavior, consistent naming, and a serviceable record of how everything is connected.

### The Control4 system, layer by layer

| Layer | What it does | What the homeowner notices | What DAVG must design correctly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Controller | Runs the Control4 project and coordinates commands, events, and compatible devices | Scenes respond and rooms behave consistently | Capacity, location, I/O, audio zones, wireless radios, expansion, and failure boundaries |
| Drivers and integrations | Translate commands and status between Control4 and third-party equipment | One interface can operate systems from different manufacturers | Driver quality, supported functions, two-way feedback, update risk, and what happens when a third-party service changes |
| Network | Carries IP communication among controllers, touchscreens, media, cameras, and connected equipment | Speed, responsiveness, remote access, and fewer intermittent failures | Switching, Wi-Fi, segmentation, addressing, power, and documentation through a **[professionally engineered home network](/solutions/infrastructure-privacy/)** |
| Interfaces | Keypads, touchscreens, Halo remotes, television interface, app, and optional voice access | The system feels obvious—or frustrating | Which interface belongs in each room, what each control exposes, and how guests use the home |
| Automation logic | Converts time, sensors, button presses, and system states into coordinated actions | Arrive, Entertain, Movie, Away, and Goodnight become reliable behaviors | Clear triggers, conditions, exceptions, manual override, naming, and testing |
| Infrastructure | Wiring, rack, power, ventilation, mounting, and pathways | Mostly invisible until something needs service or expansion | Access, labeling, spare capacity, surge/backup strategy, and trade coordination |

### Control4 controller family: how the decision is actually made

| Controller path | Manufacturer-positioned fit | Educational interpretation | DAVG decision note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focused-room or extension controller | One media room or a modest first phase | Keeps the initial scope legible without committing every room to control | Confirm current CORE model, available ports, software path and expansion plan |
| Multi-room or whole-residence controller | More sources, interfaces, audio zones and integrated subsystems | Supports one coordinated architecture across the house | Size the current CORE family to actual project and manufacturer capacity; confirm any audio routing separately |
| Additional processor or specialty design | Very large, distributed or exceptional continuity needs | May separate loads or locations for an engineered reason | Document the necessity and current manufacturer support; never select a legacy name as a prestige tier |

**Bottom line:** the most expensive controller is not automatically the best design, and the smallest controller that technically turns devices on is not automatically adequate. DAVG should size the control architecture around device count, integration complexity, audio zones, physical interfaces, property scope, continuity expectations, and future phases.

Use the [current manufacturer controller range](https://www.control4.com/solutions/products/controllers) at proposal time; older CORE/CA names in project photos can describe *that installation*, not a universal current purchasing ladder.

### What works without the internet—and what does not?

| Function | Local operation | Internet/cloud dependency to explain |
|---|---|---|
| Programmed lighting, shade, climate, and compatible device scenes | Designed to execute through the local Control4 project and local subsystem connections | Manufacturer account services, third-party cloud devices, voice services, or remote commands may be unavailable |
| Local keypads, remotes, and touchscreens | Remain the primary in-home interaction path when designed correctly | Cloud-delivered content and selected identity/account functions may not be available |
| Local media control | Control of local equipment can remain available | Streaming content still depends on internet and the streaming provider |
| Remote access and away-from-home control | Not a purely local function | Requires current Control4 service/account conditions and working internet; verify subscription requirements at proposal time |
| Notifications and cloud-backed features | Depends on feature | Explain exactly which event path is local, which leaves the property, and which third party processes it |

This table should remain visible because “local-first” does not mean “nothing uses the cloud.” The trustworthy explanation is to identify each dependency before the client buys.

## 06 — Choose the Control4 system scope

Choose the system by answering four questions before choosing products:

1. Which daily moments deserve a single command?
2. Which functions must continue without internet access?
3. Where should control live: keypad, touchscreen, phone, voice, or a deliberate mix?
4. What should be wired now so the home can change later?

The correct answer may be a focused system for a few important rooms or a complete whole-residence architecture. Coherence matters more than device count.

### Choose the first phase without trapping the second

| Homeowner situation | Likely starting brief | Test before proposing | Common expansion effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| One busy TV room, little else | Reliable source switching, tactile remote, local lighting scene | Display/AV format, source control, network, manual recovery | Save pathways and controller/rack capacity if more rooms may follow |
| Several rooms and routine actions | Rooms, subsystems, keypad/app roles and four or five household scenes | Driver support, audio zoning, shade/lighting platform, internet-loss behavior | Name and document rooms consistently so later additions fit |
| Custom home with many trades | Plan controller, network, rack, power, lighting/shades, media and interfaces with the drawing set | Ownership of each subsystem, outage behavior and coordinated rough-in | Preserve spare conduit, space and service access by design |
| Existing Control4 home | Audit the inherited project before changing hardware | Account/backup access, software, driver compatibility and undocumented wiring | Stage replacement only where evidence shows need; use the takeover audit in Section 10 |

**Operating-cost question:** Control4 states its systems [operate locally without a subscription](https://www.control4.com/services); **Control4 Connect** adds remote access and other named services under the current commercial terms. An older installation may have **4Sight** and different entitlement/history. Confirm the customer’s account, model, software version, desired remote features, renewal price and data dependencies in the proposal. Local control still depends on the local equipment, network and each subsystem; streaming, voice, third-party cloud devices and remote access can stop during an internet outage.

### Control4 versus the common alternatives

| Approach | Strength | Limitation at luxury-residence scale | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate consumer apps | Low barrier to adding one device at a time | Household logic, interfaces, accounts, updates, and failure behavior remain fragmented | A few independent devices with no whole-home expectation |
| Voice-assistant-centered setup | Convenient for simple commands | Voice is not a complete interface, is poor for discovery, and often relies on cloud services | Supplemental control for a small number of predictable actions |
| Manufacturer-specific subsystem apps | Often expose deep settings for one category | Lighting, shades, audio, climate, and security remain separate experiences | Specialist setup and occasional advanced adjustment |
| Professionally integrated Control4 | One programmed architecture can coordinate many compatible systems and interface types | Requires disciplined design, professional programming, a stable network, and long-term documentation | Residences where multiple systems, rooms, users, and project phases must behave coherently |

### Which Control4 interface belongs where?

| Interface | Strongest use | Where it becomes the wrong choice |
|---|---|---|
| Engraved keypad | Repeated room actions: scenes, shades, music, away, and goodnight | Too many buttons or vague labels turn it into a control puzzle |
| T4 touchscreen | Shared spaces that need discovery, status, intercom, cameras, media, and deeper control | Unnecessary in every room; wall placement and power/network must be resolved early |
| Halo / Halo Touch remote | Media rooms requiring tactile, one-handed AV control plus access to broader home functions | It should not become the only way to control lights or shades in the room |
| Control4 app | Personal control, search, status, and away-from-home access where service conditions allow | Phones are poor primary controls for guests, children, and repeated physical actions |
| Television interface | Occasional whole-home navigation from a display | Not appropriate for quick room actions or as the only interface |
| Voice | Convenience for a small set of memorable commands | Privacy, ambiguity, noise, cloud dependency, and discoverability make it supplemental rather than foundational |

### What a high-value Control4 design includes that a device quote does not

- A room-by-room interface schedule explaining exactly what people touch.
- A scene and state schedule written in household language.
- A supported-integration list that distinguishes full two-way control from limited command-only behavior.
- A network and equipment architecture with ownership, power, cooling, segmentation, and service access.
- A new-construction, retrofit, or takeover plan with clear exclusions.
- Testing for internet outage, device failure, manual override, guest use, and conflicting commands.
- Final documentation: controller, drivers, device names, IP plan, cable labels, rack elevations, backups, and account ownership.
- A change process so future upgrades do not quietly break existing behavior.

### Ten Control4 features worth designing deliberately

1. **Favorites:** Put the household’s actual rooms, scenes, cameras, stations, and sources within immediate reach instead of exposing every possible command.
2. **Lighting scenes:** Coordinate **[architectural lighting](/solutions/architectural-lighting/)** by activity and time, not by a long list of loads.
3. **Shade coordination:** Join glare, privacy, and media states through **[motorized shading](/solutions/motorized-shades/)** without removing local control.
4. **Watch and Listen:** Make source selection, room routing, volume, and power sequence predictable through **[distributed media and audio](/solutions/media-audio/)**.
5. **Security awareness:** Surface appropriate cameras, locks, and entry actions without exposing administrative complexity through **[cameras and controlled entry](/solutions/security-access/)**.
6. **Climate integration:** Present useful setpoints, modes, and schedules without pretending Control4 replaces mechanical design.
7. **Notifications:** Reserve alerts for events worth interrupting someone; define recipient, timing, and escalation.
8. **Occupancy and sensors:** Use inputs as context, with time, mode, and manual actions preventing unwanted automation.
9. **Away and Goodnight:** Confirm the systems that matter, expose exceptions, and avoid irreversible “all off” logic.
10. **Personalization:** Give different rooms and users relevant access while keeping the shared household experience consistent.

## 09 — New build, retrofit or system takeover

**New construction:** Begin during schematic design so keypad locations, low-voltage pathways, rack space, shade power, and subsystem interfaces can enter the drawing set before framing and electrical decisions close.  
**Renovation:** Coordinate around demolition and finish schedules, preserving walls while taking advantage of newly accessible pathways.  
**Retrofit:** Audit existing wiring, network health, attic/crawlspace access, and compatible devices before choosing a wired, wireless, or hybrid route.  
**System takeover:** Document the inherited system first; retain what is sound, isolate what is fragile, and establish a realistic migration plan.

## 10 — Prevent frustrating control failures

- **Too many apps:** prevent this by defining one control architecture and a clear source of truth.
- **Automations nobody trusts:** use a small number of understandable scenes with manual override.
- **Cloud-dependent basics:** keep essential in-home operation local wherever the selected systems support it.
- **Weak network underneath premium devices:** engineer infrastructure before layering on control.
- **Late keypad and rack decisions:** coordinate locations while plans and walls are still open.
- **Undocumented programming:** preserve device, network, cable, and scene documentation at handoff.

### Control4 takeover: what DAVG must inspect before promising an upgrade

An inherited Control4 system may be fundamentally sound, partially documented, constrained by aging infrastructure, or dependent on integrations that have changed. The first step is not replacing the controller. It is establishing ownership and truth.

| Audit area | What DAVG checks | Why it changes the recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Project and account ownership | Dealer relationship, owner access, backups, credentials, licenses, and service status | A technically functional system may still be difficult to support if ownership is unclear |
| Controller and software generation | Main controller, secondary controllers, project size, software path, and connected radios | Determines compatibility, migration sequence, and which devices can remain |
| Network | Gateway, switches, access points, addressing, segmentation, remote management, and intermittent faults | Many “automation problems” are actually network or power problems |
| Drivers and third-party systems | Driver source, version, supported feedback, account dependency, and update history | A single obsolete integration can drive a larger migration than the visible device suggests |
| Interfaces | Keypads, touchscreens, remotes, apps, labeling, and household pain points | Reveals whether the issue is hardware, programming, training, or all three |
| Infrastructure | Rack, heat, power, UPS, cable condition, labeling, and spare pathways | Establishes whether the upgraded system will remain serviceable |

The persuasive promise is not “we can make anything work.” It is that DAVG will determine what is worth preserving, explain the risk of each migration path, and avoid charging the client to replace good infrastructure without evidence.

Denver-area projects often combine high solar exposure, large areas of glass, outdoor living, renovations of finished homes, and long distances between main living spaces and property features. Those conditions connect control decisions to shading, climate, Wi-Fi, landscape lighting, and exterior audio earlier than many owners expect.

**What does a Control4 system actually control?**  
A Control4 controller can coordinate compatible lighting, climate, media, shades and selected security/access functions through programmed scenes and several interface types. DAVG specifies each supported driver and control depth, then tests the local and cloud-dependent actions. A new build can plan pathways early; a finished home requires a wiring, RF and device-compatibility survey.

**Do I need a subscription for it to work?**  
Control4 says its system can operate locally without a subscription. Its current Connect service adds remote access and selected other services; legacy 4Sight systems can have different terms. The proposal should itemize the functions requiring Connect, broadband, third-party accounts or streaming. No integrator should claim *every* function is local without auditing the specific design. [Control4 services](https://www.control4.com/services).

**What happens if my internet goes down?**  
Locally programmed actions may still work when the controller, local network, power and relevant subsystem remain available. Remote access, streaming and cloud-only voice/devices generally require an outside service. DAVG tests the actual outage case rather than promising an invisible interruption.

**Can this be added to a home that's already built?**  
Often, yes. DAVG first surveys line/low-voltage wiring, network, radio coverage, equipment access and the existing systems. A finished home may use compatible wireless controls and targeted cable runs; a new build can place the right power, signal and service paths during rough-in. Cat6A is a network cable choice, not a universal keypad or sensor requirement.

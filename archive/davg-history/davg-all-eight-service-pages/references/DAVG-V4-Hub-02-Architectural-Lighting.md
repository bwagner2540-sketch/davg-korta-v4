## 02 — What is architectural lighting control?

Architectural lighting is the coordinated design of fixture layers, circuits, controls, and scenes around how a space should look and work. DAVG translates the design intent into a control architecture that gives each layer a purpose and makes the finished room simple to operate.

Good lighting control does more than dim. It preserves the relationship between ambient, task, accent, decorative, and exterior light while replacing switch-bank complexity with clear, repeatable scenes.

## 03 — The room through the day

Morning can feel open and useful; evening can become warmer and quieter; artwork, stone, timber, and landscape can hold their intended depth after daylight fades. The household chooses a scene, not a row of individual circuits.

The goal is consistency without rigidity: the design establishes the best default states, while local control remains intuitive whenever someone wants to change them.

## 04 — Fixtures, loads, scenes and control

The system joins load schedules, dimming methods, fixture compatibility, panel or local controls, keypad locations, scene logic, occupancy inputs, daylight response, and integration with shades and whole-home control. ## 06 — Choose a Lutron platform and keypad

Decide in this order:

1. What should the architecture reveal after daylight fades?
2. Which activities need distinct scenes?
3. Which fixtures require specialized dimming or tunable control?
4. Where should keypads replace banks of switches?
5. Should the platform be optimized for an existing home or designed into new construction?

The platform tier follows the project’s circuit count, integration depth, construction stage, and design ambition.

### Lutron platform comparison: which level fits the residence?

| Platform | Strongest fit | Control architecture | Design implication | When to move up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Caséta | Focused retrofit rooms and smaller residential scopes | Wireless control designed around a limited, accessible ecosystem | Preserves existing wiring and minimizes disruption | When the project needs deeper whole-home zoning, broader keypad strategy, or more integrated scenes |
| RadioRA 3 | Whole-home retrofit and renovation where wireless flexibility matters | Professional whole-home wireless lighting control with more scene and keypad capability | Strong path for finished residences that need consistency without centralized panel work | When architecture, load count, keypad density, or advanced integration calls for HomeWorks |
| HomeWorks QSX | Custom whole-home new construction, major renovation, and high-design residences | Lutron’s highest residential control tier with deep zoning and architectural control options | Best opportunity to coordinate panels, keypads, fixtures, shades, and design intent from the drawings forward | Chosen when the project—not prestige—requires its scale, finish, and integration depth |

**Bottom line:** the decision is not “good, better, best” in isolation. It is the fit between construction stage, electrical topology, fixture/load requirements, keypad design, shade integration, and the number of rooms that must behave consistently.

### Start at the ceiling and load schedule, then choose the platform

1. **Define the visual result:** a kitchen needs working light, a living room may need low-glare indirect light and art accent, and a corridor needs safe circulation. Mark ambient, task, accent and decorative sources on the reflected-ceiling plan.
2. **Choose the fixture and driver together:** record source, optic, trim, color temperature, color quality, driver, dimming protocol and expected low-end behavior for every controlled load. Warm dimming changes appearance with dim level; tunable white allows a separate color-temperature command and requires compatible source, driver and controls. Neither is automatically beneficial in every room.
3. **Group loads around useful scenes:** document which luminaires move together, which need separate aiming or dimming, which remain manual, and what must operate when a processor/network is offline. Obtain the electrician’s circuit and panel schedule.
4. **Select control topology:** local wallbox dimming may minimize retrofit disruption; panelized or hybrid HomeWorks designs can reduce switch banks and coordinate a more complex new-build load plan. Verify neutral, box depth, line voltage, control wire, RF environment and code requirements for the *exact* devices. HomeWorks is not synonymous with hardwired communication only.
5. **Choose the platform and wall controls:** Caséta for focused simpler scopes, RadioRA 3 for a professional distributed whole-home path, HomeWorks for demanding architectural, fixture and system integration. Confirm supported devices, capacity and dealer programming before promising a keypad or third-party scene.

Reference: [Lutron’s residential system selector](https://support.lutron.com/us/en/product/radiora3/article/system-design-setup/Which-Lutron-Residential-System-is-Best-for-Me) and [LED compatibility guidance](https://assets.lutron.com/a/documents/048579.pdf). A fixture’s claim to be “dimmable” is insufficient evidence for a pleasing low end with the selected dimmer.

### Lutron architectural-control comparison

| Control family | Visual character | Best use | Buyer decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alisse | Hand-finished architectural wall control with a decorative metal presence | Interiors where the control should read as a deliberate jewelry-like object | Choose finish, button configuration, engraving, and wall relationship with the interior designer |
| Palladiom keypad | Minimal, precise architectural control with restrained geometry | Contemporary spaces where controls should align quietly with stone, plaster, millwork, or metal | Coordinate finish samples, engraving language, backbox, elevation, and nearby electrical devices |
| Sunnata keypad / dimmer | Tactile, contemporary wall controls in supported RadioRA 3 or HomeWorks designs | Renovations and rooms needing simple access to a few clear scenes | Confirm exact model/platform, load role and finish with the integrator |
| seeTouch / other HomeWorks controls | Familiar multi-button functionality across broad project conditions | Rooms needing clear scene access without the same decorative-object emphasis | Keep button count and naming disciplined; familiarity does not excuse clutter |
| Companion app/touchscreen | Deep control, editing, discovery, and whole-home status | Occasional adjustment and system-wide navigation | It supports the room; it does not replace an obvious physical control at the point of use |

Alisse and Palladiom are architectural [HomeWorks keypad families](https://www.lutron.com/us/en/controls/keypads-remotes); they are not merely interchangeable decorative faceplates for Caséta or RadioRA 3. An engraved keypad can put **Entertain**, **Evening**, **All Off** and a supported shade action at the entry, but button count, press/hold behavior, status indicators, engraving and companion control should be tested on a room elevation. A scene keypad simplifies *use* after the electrical loads and programming have been designed; it does not erase electrical circuits.

### What DAVG evaluates before approving a fixture and dimming path

- Fixture source and driver type; forward-phase, reverse-phase, 0–10 V, digital, or other control requirements.
- Minimum and maximum dimming behavior, flicker, shimmer, pop-on, drop-out, and audible noise.
- Color consistency across fixtures and the visible change at low output.
- Load grouping, emergency or code requirements, and how circuits align with actual room use.
- Keypad locations and engravings in relation to doors, millwork, wall finishes, and adjacent devices.
- Whether tunable-white or color control provides a meaningful design outcome or adds complexity without benefit.
- How **[motorized shading](/solutions/motorized-shades/)** changes daylight and scene requirements.

### Questions a sophisticated homeowner should ask

1. Has the exact fixture-driver-control combination been tested, or is compatibility assumed from a general list?
2. Who owns the final load schedule and scene schedule?
3. Will the electrician receive panel, wiring, keypad, and control-interface requirements before rough-in?
4. What happens at the wall if the processor or network is unavailable?
5. How are scenes commissioned after finishes, furniture, and artwork are installed?
6. Which adjustments can the owner make, and which require the integrator?
7. How will replacement drivers or fixtures be matched years later?

## 09 — New construction, renovation or retrofit

**New construction:** coordinate fixture specifications, panel strategy, keypad elevations, shade pockets, and control wiring before electrical rough-in.  
**Renovation:** use opened ceilings and walls to rationalize circuits and reduce future visual clutter.  
**Retrofit:** retain existing line-voltage wiring where appropriate and choose a control path compatible with the home’s actual electrical conditions.  
**Existing-system update:** audit loads, processors, keypads, programming, and documentation before defining a staged migration.

## 10 — Prevent dimming and scene mistakes

- **Incompatible fixture and dimmer pairings:** test and specify the full control chain.
- **Too many keypad buttons:** name a small set of actions people understand instantly.
- **Flat lighting:** separate ambient, task, and accent layers instead of dimming one circuit.
- **Late shade coordination:** align pockets, wiring, and light-control intent together.
- **Scenes designed from a desk:** commission in the completed room, after finishes and furnishings are present.
- **Exterior lighting on a separate timer:** bring it into the same scheduling and scene logic.

### The difference between dimming and lighting design

A dimmer changes output. Lighting design decides what should be illuminated, from where, with what beam, at what color and intensity, and in relationship to every other layer. Control then preserves those relationships as usable scenes.

This distinction matters commercially: premium controls cannot rescue poor fixture placement, incompatible drivers, or a room built from one flat layer. DAVG’s role is to make the control architecture support the lighting and interior design—not substitute for them.

## 12 — What changes lighting investment?

Key variables are controlled-load count, keypad count, panelized versus local architecture, fixture and driver compatibility, tunable-light requirements, engraving, exterior zones, integration depth, construction access, and commissioning time.

A useful proposal begins with a fixture schedule or room-by-room load study. Generic per-square-foot pricing hides the decisions that actually shape the system.

**Relative path:** adapting a few compatible existing loads and wallboxes is a different construction scope from a whole-home RadioRA 3 retrofit; a panelized or hybrid HomeWorks project adds early panel/wiring coordination and can shift costs from visible wall hardware to infrastructure. Decorative Alisse/Palladiom controls, custom fixture/driver matching, tunable light and scene commissioning can each move the budget independently. Present a DAVG-approved installed range only after the actual load and fixture schedules are known; show what the proposed control tier includes and excludes.

## 13 — Scene commissioning and adjustment

Begin with the architectural and interior-design intent. Build the load and scene schedule. Coordinate with the lighting designer and electrician. Confirm samples and compatibility. Install and program. Then commission in the finished space, documenting scenes, loads, keypad functions, and future adjustment paths.

Lighting should be revisited after the household has lived with it; small scene refinements often matter more than adding more controls.

## 14 — Denver light and homeowner questions

Colorado’s strong daylight, high solar exposure, large window walls, and rapid evening transitions make daylight, shading, and electric light one combined design problem. Exterior lighting must also support year-round use without overpowering the landscape or neighboring properties.

### Architectural lighting by location

Connect first to **[lighting design across the Denver area](/areas-served/)**. **What's the difference between Lutron Caséta, RadioRA 3, and HomeWorks QSX?**  
Caséta serves a focused, simpler scope; RadioRA 3 supports a professionally designed whole-home wireless-control path; HomeWorks supports extensive architectural lighting and integration with both wired and supported wireless options. The right choice depends on load types, wallbox/panel strategy, finish and keypad goals, renovation access and expected scale. DAVG confirms exact model and capacity rather than treating the three as purely price tiers.

**Can this be added to existing wiring, or does it need new construction?**  
Both are viable, subject to a load/wiring survey. Existing wallboxes can sometimes support local dimming or wireless communication; a custom HomeWorks panel or new control wire is easier to coordinate while walls are open. No RF device eliminates the need for compatible electrical wiring, driver and suitable box conditions.

**Does Lutron integrate with Control4, or run separately?**  
Supported Lutron platforms and Control4 drivers can coordinate lighting and shades with broader household scenes. Exact integration functions, feedback, keypad behavior and any network/cloud dependency depend on the selected Lutron system, Control4 version and driver. DAVG tests the promised actions on the proposed combination.

**Can this control landscape and exterior lighting too?**  
They can be part of a coordinated lighting plan when the exterior loads, drivers and controls are compatible. An astronomical schedule or evening scene may simplify use; DAVG confirms the exterior power/control arrangement and coordinates aiming and neighborhood impact.

**What is the difference between warm dimming and tunable white?**  
Warm dimming follows a designed warmer color as output falls; tunable white gives compatible fixtures a separate white-color setting. Both depend on the source, driver and control protocol. A sample room at low light tells more than the name on a specification sheet.

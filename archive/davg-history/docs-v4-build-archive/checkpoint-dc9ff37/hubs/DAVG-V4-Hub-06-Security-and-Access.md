## 02 — What should security actually cover?

Residential security design determines what the system should see, what it should ignore, who can enter, how events are recorded, and who can access that information. DAVG connects camera placement, access points, storage, networking, power, control, and household preferences into one documented plan.

The right system reduces blind spots and meaningless alerts while making ordinary access—family, guests, service providers—clearer to manage.

## 03 — Alerts, visitors and privacy

An alert should explain something relevant, not merely announce motion. A visitor can be seen before the door is opened. Temporary access can be deliberate and time-bound. Leaving the home can coordinate locks, selected lights, and the appropriate security state without a sequence of separate apps.

Privacy choices should be explicit: where cameras do and do not look, whether recording is local or cloud-based, how long footage is retained, and who receives access.

## 04 — Property, storage and access architecture

The architecture joins property boundaries, approach paths, entries, fields of view, mounting heights, low-light conditions, recorder or cloud model, storage duration, access credentials, locks or gates, intercom, network segmentation, power backup, remote access, user roles, and integrations.

Coverage and privacy are designed together. More cameras are not automatically a better system.

## 06 — Choose events, cameras and credentials

Decide:

1. Which events must be visible, and at what level of detail?
2. Which areas must never be recorded?
3. Local storage, cloud service, or a deliberate hybrid?
4. Who gets live view, playback, administrative control, and temporary entry permissions?
5. What must continue during internet or utility interruption?
6. Which security events should interact with lighting or whole-home control?

### Local recorder, cloud service, or hybrid?

| Recording model | Strength | Tradeoff | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local network video recorder (NVR) | Owner-selected on-site storage, local recording continuity, and direct control of retention capacity | Recorder, drives, network, backup power, cooling, remote-access security, and physical protection must be designed and maintained | Privacy-conscious residences that want clear on-premise custody and adequate equipment space |
| Cloud recording | Off-site service, simpler remote access, and reduced on-site recorder responsibility | Subscription, internet dependency, third-party data handling, retention limits, and account governance | Smaller or focused systems where service convenience outweighs local-custody priorities |
| Hybrid | Local continuity plus selected off-site access or event handling | Most complex policy: owners must understand what is stored where and under which account | Residences requiring local recording and a carefully bounded remote layer |

**Bottom line:** storage is a privacy and continuity decision, not a checkbox. DAVG should document retention, administrator roles, remote access, account ownership, and outage behavior before the hardware is approved.

### Size retention and continuity rather than guessing a recorder

For each camera, record resolution, frame rate, codec, motion/continuous schedule and expected scene activity; estimate total storage from those settings and the requested retention period, with capacity for health events and a documented overwrite/export policy. Local recording can continue without outside internet **only if** the camera, local switch, recorder and power remain operational; remote viewing or cloud backup needs additional services. Test a simulated broadband interruption and the agreed power-backup duration. Explain who can export footage and when data leaves the property.

### Camera selection: the specification is the scene

| Factor | Why it matters | Buyer-facing question |
|---|---|---|
| Field of view | A wide scene provides context but may sacrifice identifying detail at the target | What must be recognized, and at what exact distance? |
| Resolution and pixel density | Resolution only helps when enough pixels cover the person, plate, package, or event area | Has the system been designed around target detail rather than camera megapixels alone? |
| Lens and mounting position | Lens, angle, height, and distance determine faces, glare, occlusion, and perspective | Will the camera see a face or mostly the top of a head? |
| Low-light and supplemental light | Night performance depends on the scene, reflective surfaces, landscape light, and camera behavior | Has the view been tested after dark with the finished lighting? |
| Detection/classification | Can reduce irrelevant motion alerts when configured for the scene | Which classifications are supported on the exact camera/recorder, and where is analysis performed? |
| Environmental rating | Weather resistance does not correct poor mounting, sealing, drainage, or sun exposure | Is the entire installation detail suitable for the location? |

**Define the detection task at each point.** “Observe the driveway,” “recognize a known visitor at the front approach,” and “identify an unfamiliar person at the gate” have different image-detail requirements. Draw target distance, lens angle, target pixel density, backlight/night lighting and any permitted privacy mask on each view. Confirm *day and night* at the actual installation height. A camera that sees a large yard is not proof it can read a plate or identify a face at its far edge.

### Access-control product paths

| Path | What it solves | What it does not solve |
|---|---|---|
| Video doorbell / entry camera | Visitor awareness and two-way communication | It does not manage every credential, gate, or controlled opening |
| Smart residential lock | Keyless household entry and selected temporary credentials | It may not satisfy broader gate, multi-door, power, or hardware requirements |
| Integrated door/gate access | Coordinated credentials, door/gate state, intercom, and **[home intelligence scenes](/solutions/home-intelligence/)** | It requires early hardware, power, network, safety, and trade coordination |

## 07 — Property-specific coverage examples

| Property moment | Camera/access design question | Privacy and failure question |
|---|---|---|
| Driveway/gate approach | Is a person/vehicle visible early enough, and what detail is needed at the entry point? | What is recorded across a public road or neighbor boundary; what if broadband or gate power fails? |
| Front door/package | Can a visitor be seen face-on in daylight and after dark, and can the doorbell reach the household? | Who receives an alert and can replay it; does entry still work with an ordinary key or approved manual route? |
| Garage/detached structure | Does the camera cover doors and approach without broadcasting household interiors? | Are local network, power and backup connections present across the distance? |
| Cleaner/guest/dog walker | What door, hours and revocation are needed? | Does the chosen credential *actually* expire on schedule, and who can revoke it? |
| Pool/family patio | Is this zone intentionally excluded or narrowly framed for a stated purpose? | Record privacy masks, account rights and retention limits rather than assuming detection is harmless |

These examples become a property-specific coverage/permissions matrix. Keep public visuals generalized and avoid exposing the installed security plan.

## 09 — Entry, gate and retrofit routes

**New construction:** coordinate conduit, cable, entry hardware, gate control, mounting blocks, power, network, and equipment locations before exterior finishes.  
**Renovation:** use access created by facade, door, gate, or electrical work to improve placement and concealment.  
**Retrofit:** survey sightlines and wire paths; avoid choosing locations solely because they are easy to reach.  
**Takeover:** change credentials, document users, inspect firmware and network placement, test recording, and confirm ownership of any cloud account before expanding the system.

## 10 — Prevent blind spots and nuisance alerts

- **Blind spots hidden by wide-angle marketing:** design for the detail needed at each specific target.
- **Constant nuisance alerts:** define detection zones, classifications, schedules, and notification roles.
- **Cameras on the household’s primary network:** use deliberate segmentation and access rules.
- **Unclear account ownership:** establish owner-controlled credentials and documented administrators.
- **Entry devices added after door hardware:** coordinate access control with doors, gates, power, and life-safety requirements.
- **Privacy treated as a default setting:** document exclusions, retention, and access explicitly.

## 12 — What changes security investment?

Scope is shaped by property area, target zones, image-detail requirements, night conditions, camera and entry count, door/gate hardware, local storage duration, network and power needs, trenching or difficult cable routes, integration, and ongoing cloud or monitoring choices.

The page should explain these variables without publishing package prices or promising that a system prevents crime, protects data absolutely, or qualifies for an insurance outcome.

**Relative scope:** a single entry camera and compatible lock is different from a multi-camera property with local recorder, gate intercom, trenching, backup power and managed credentials. Show separate camera/view, recording/storage, door/gate hardware, network/power, cabling/construction, subscription and lifecycle costs. Installation price and ongoing recording/account costs should be visible in the same comparison, with approved installed ranges inserted only from project estimates.

## 13 — Roles, updates and owner handoff

Begin with a privacy and threat-priority conversation. Walk the property. Produce coverage and access studies. Confirm storage, user roles, network isolation, power continuity, and integrations. Coordinate doors, gates, lighting, landscape, structure, and finishes. Install, aim, commission, test day/night behavior, document access, and train owners. **Is footage stored locally or in the cloud, and who has access?**  
Both models and hybrids exist. Local storage stays on the selected recorder only to the extent that the camera, integration and remote-view paths actually keep it there; cloud service may handle events or remote access even with a local recorder. DAVG documents retention, exports, administrator roles, service fees, backup and outage behavior before the owner chooses.

**What separates a video doorbell from full access control?**  
A video doorbell is primarily a visitor camera and communication point; a compatible lock or access system controls entry permissions. A gate or multiple doors can add intercom, door hardware, release, power and safety coordination. Some homes need only one of those pieces; DAVG designs from the entrances and users.

**Can temporary access be granted without a physical key?**  
Selected locks/access platforms support scheduled or revocable codes/credentials, but expiration, offline behavior and account control depend on the exact device and service. DAVG demonstrates granting and revoking access and documents the physical/manual entry route before relying on it for a guest or service provider.

**How is a connected camera system actually secured against being hacked?**  
Security comes from multiple maintained controls: unique credentials and MFA where supported, firmware updates, encrypted connections when available, limited user permissions, documented remote-access paths and network separation where useful. A VLAN alone cannot guarantee security; rules and allowed integrations have to be checked. [FTC camera guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-secure-your-home-security-cameras) and [NIST connected-home guidance](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/7-tips-keep-your-smart-home-safer-and-more-private-nist-cybersecurity) inform the owner handoff.

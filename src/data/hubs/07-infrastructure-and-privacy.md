## 02 — What is the home network responsible for?

Digital infrastructure is the physical and logical foundation that carries communication, media, control, and security through the residence. DAVG designs the wired network, wireless coverage, equipment environment, power strategy, segmentation, and documentation around the home’s construction and actual device load.

Fast internet service is only the connection to the property. The experience inside the home still depends on how well signal, traffic, and equipment are managed after that connection arrives.

## 03 — Stable work, streaming and control

Calls remain stable while other rooms stream. Control responds without hesitation. Cameras and connected devices do not share unrestricted access with personal computers. Outdoor living areas have coverage designed for them. Equipment is labeled, ventilated, protected, and serviceable.

The network should be mostly invisible to the household and highly legible to the people responsible for maintaining it.

## 04 — Internet handoff, switching and Wi-Fi

The architecture joins the internet handoff, gateway and firewall, switching, access points, structured cabling, fiber or copper backhaul where appropriate, VLANs, device addressing, rack and ventilation, UPS and power protection, monitoring choices, outdoor coverage, remote-support boundaries, and documentation.

Coverage is shaped by materials and geometry. Capacity is shaped by clients, traffic, and concurrent use. Privacy is shaped by segmentation, credentials, updates, ownership, and policy—not a single product badge.

**Follow the fault boundary:** ISP/broadband handoff → gateway/firewall → switch and wired backhaul → access point and RF → client device/application. “Fast service, weak Wi-Fi” can mean an RF or internal-network issue; “strong Wi-Fi bars, slow pages” can mean the outside circuit, DNS, gateway, congestion or application. Measure at more than one layer before selling more bandwidth or more access points.

## 06 — Plan coverage and network separation

Start with:

1. Construction materials, floor count, site area, and outdoor coverage.
2. Current and planned devices by function—not one undifferentiated total.
3. Spaces where latency, throughput, or continuity matter most.
4. Systems that should be isolated from personal or work devices.
5. Cable and equipment paths that can be built now for future change.
6. Ownership, documentation, update, and remote-access expectations.

### Consumer mesh versus engineered residential networking

| Design area | Consumer mesh approach | Engineered residential approach |
|---|---|---|
| Access-point placement | Usually chosen around available outlets and trial-and-error signal | Planned from construction materials, floor geometry, client density, roaming, and priority rooms |
| Backhaul | May rely heavily on wireless hops | Uses wired Ethernet or fiber backhaul where practical to preserve predictable capacity |
| Switching and power | Minimal or embedded | Sized PoE switching, uplinks, power budget, rack, cooling, UPS, and service access |
| Segmentation | Basic guest network or limited isolation | Deliberate separation of personal, work, guest, camera, automation, and connected-device traffic where required |
| Visibility and service | Consumer app with limited topology/detail | Documented topology, controlled administration, logs/monitoring options, configuration records, and ownership |
| Best fit | Smaller homes with light device load and modest service expectations | Larger or material-dense homes carrying automation, security, media, work, and outdoor coverage |

The category labels are planning examples. A wired consumer system can outperform an improperly placed “professional” kit; an engineered design is the placement, topology, measurement and service plan, not the logo on the access point.

### Prewire by endpoint, not by a single cable slogan

| Destination | Typical pathway decision | What is checked before rough-in |
|---|---|---|
| Access point, camera or network endpoint | Properly rated copper network cable, often Cat6A when length, pathway and future capacity justify it, plus PoE budget where supported | Link distance, environment, termination, switch capacity, location and service loop |
| Separate building or long/hostile route | Fiber and appropriate conduit can provide distance and electrical isolation where designed correctly | Local power, fiber type/termination, equipment, grounding/surge coordination and future pulls |
| Passive speaker | Appropriately sized/approved speaker cable from the specified amplifier path | Channel, load, run length, fire rating and location; network cable is not a substitute |
| Display, cinema or specialty system | Product-specific signal/control/power plus a serviceable conduit where valuable | HDMI/format distance, source location, mount support, ventilation and future replacement |
| Rack | ISP entry, patch/switch, equipment power, cooling and physical access | Space/heat, labeling, UPS runtime, shutdown/startup order and who owns credentials |

Document measured cable routes and local electrical/low-voltage requirements with the trades. No default “Cat6A everywhere” claim guarantees a future use or an installation price.

### What “privacy” means on this page

Privacy is not a claim that the residence cannot be compromised. It is a set of design and ownership decisions: limit unnecessary exposure, separate device classes, use unique credentials, define administrators, keep firmware and configuration governed, document third-party services, and make remote access explicit.

### What DAVG measures and tests

- Internet handoff and realistic service performance separately from internal network performance.
- Wired cable condition, topology, termination, link speed, PoE demand, and spare pathways.
- RF conditions by room, floor, material, exterior transition, and priority use—not signal bars at one moment.
- Concurrent streaming, calls, automation, cameras, and roaming at realistic demand.
- VLAN and firewall behavior without breaking required discovery or integration.
- Rack temperature, power events, startup order, labeling, backups, and administrator ownership.
- Dependencies for **[home intelligence](/solutions/home-intelligence/)**, **[camera and access-control traffic](/solutions/security-access/)**, and **[distributed media systems](/solutions/media-audio/)**.

## 07 — What a serviceable network provides

| Residence/use condition | What to measure | Likely design emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Finished multi-level home with one dead room | Wired and Wi-Fi speed separately, RSSI/roaming, wall/floor attenuation | Place a cabled access point where feasible; compare a less-invasive mesh route if wiring costs dominate |
| Custom new build with masonry and broad glass | RF through final materials, room/device demand, cable runs and outdoor transition | Prewire AP locations and fixed endpoints, plan rack/PoE and leave serviceable pathways |
| Work-from-home plus media and cameras | Calls while video/recording run, internal switch performance and WAN behavior | Prioritize stable wired workstation paths, adequate Wi-Fi capacity and camera isolation where useful |
| Detached office and exterior terrace | Distance, outdoor exposure, existing conduit and local power | Design fiber/copper path and exterior access points from actual property and electrical conditions |

For each, hand over a topology, AP and endpoint schedule, administrator/guest policy, backup/configuration record and owner contacts. A network is tested with realistic household traffic, not only an empty-home speed test.

## 09 — New wiring, retrofit or takeover

**New construction:** plan telecom entry, rack, conduit, cable routes, access-point locations, exterior coverage, power, cooling, and service clearance before rough-in.  
**Renovation:** use open walls and ceilings to correct cable topology and move equipment to a serviceable environment.  
**Retrofit:** survey signal and materials, test existing cable, identify reachable pathways, and design a wired-first hybrid where practical.  
**Network takeover:** secure ownership, change administrative credentials, document topology, update carefully, and stage changes to avoid unnecessary household disruption.

## 10 — Why more internet speed may not fix Wi-Fi

- **Buying more internet speed to fix poor coverage:** diagnose the inside network and RF conditions separately.
- **Access points placed for convenience:** place from a material-aware coverage study and wire the backhaul where possible.
- **Everything on one flat network:** separate device classes according to the project’s risk and operational needs.
- **Rack in a hot, inaccessible closet:** design power, cooling, clearance, and service paths.
- **Unlabeled cables and unknown accounts:** document physical and logical ownership at handoff.
- **Automatic changes with no rollback plan:** govern updates and configuration backups deliberately.

## 12 — What changes network investment?

Scope follows floor and site area, construction materials, access-point and wired-port count, existing cable condition, rack relocation or buildout, switching and power requirements, outdoor coverage, segmentation complexity, trenching, integration load, and documentation needs.

Avoid promises of complete security, guaranteed coverage, or a device-capacity number detached from the selected system and environment.

**Relative scope:** tuning an existing small home may need placement and configuration; a material-heavy renovation can require new AP backhaul and rack cleanup; a new large property can add structured wiring, fiber to outbuildings, PoE, UPS and outdoor coverage. Quote the measured coverage goals and separate broadband service fees, hardware, pathways/construction, configuration/segmentation and ongoing support. Publish installed bands only from DAVG estimates and state what materials/outbuildings they include.

## 14 — Denver construction materials and questions

Masonry, tile, concrete, low-emissivity glazing, radiant barriers, mechanical rooms, detached structures, and long indoor-outdoor transitions all influence Front Range residential coverage. A plan that works in an open suburban model home may not work in a mountain-view residence with dense materials and broad exterior spaces.

**Why isn't the router from my internet provider enough?**  
It may be enough for a smaller layout and modest demands. In a larger or material-heavy home, one device may not cover every room or support the preferred segmentation and service tools. DAVG tests the broadband handoff, wired performance, Wi-Fi coverage and simultaneous use before recommending a gateway or AP change.

**Will mesh Wi-Fi actually eliminate dead zones in a large home?**  
Sometimes. Mesh nodes need adequate signal to one another; thick materials and long spans can constrain their wireless backhaul. Wired access points often give more predictable capacity when cable routes exist. DAVG surveys priority rooms and tests after installation rather than promising zero dead zones from a kit name.

**Does a smart home need a separate network for cameras and IoT devices?**  
Segmentation is often useful for limiting which devices can reach personal/work systems, but it requires correct firewall rules and allowances for needed discovery/control. Some small installations need simpler boundaries. DAVG documents access policy, credentials, firmware and remote paths; a VLAN reduces certain exposures but is not a security guarantee. [NIST connected-home guidance](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/7-tips-keep-your-smart-home-safer-and-more-private-nist-cybersecurity).

**What actually separates consumer mesh gear from a professional network?**  
The useful difference is a documented design and support path: measured AP placement, backhaul, PoE and power capacity, controlled administration, segmentation where justified and a realistic test. Brand or “enterprise” labeling alone does not prove better coverage. A consumer kit can be appropriate for a small finished home.
